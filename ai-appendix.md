Reply: Response to Reviewer: Parallel Trends and Identification I accept that parallel trends cannot be proven, and I accept two smaller points: the e = -1 baseline shifts every coefficient, and the end of the event window is thin. But none of these supports the conclusion that the estimate is uninformative. These are my three objections, ranked by how much damage they do to that conclusion.

The reviewer’s own noise yardstick shows the post-period effect is far too large to be a trend violation (most damaging). The paragraph says pre-period deviations of 0.03 to 0.85 are what shared shocks across eight boards would produce by chance. I accept that scale as the measure of how much differential movement these boards generate absent treatment. Against it, the estimates climb from 1.4 at e = 0 to 26.6 at e = 8, which is about 30 times the largest pre-period deviation. For parallel trends to fail enough to produce this, the untreated paths of adopting boards would need to diverge from comparison boards by an order of magnitude more than anything in the pre-period. The divergence would also have to start at adoption and grow in step with exposure. Noise does not produce a monotone ramp. This is a quantitative question, so the response should treat it as one. I will report a sensitivity analysis in the Rambachan-Roth style, bounding post-period violations at multiples of the largest pre-period deviation and reporting the breakdown multiple at which the confidence interval first includes zero. If that multiple is large, the estimate is informative even under partial identification, which is exactly what “uninformative” denies. The reviewer needs to show that a violation of plausible size can generate this pattern, not just that one is conceivable.
“Not proven” does not imply “uninformative,” and the paragraph concedes as much. The reviewer’s argument that parallel trends is an untestable post-period assumption applies to every difference-in-differences design ever estimated. If that were sufficient, no such estimate could ever be informative, and the critique would say nothing specific about this programme. The paragraph also concedes that the pre-period makes the assumption “more or less credible,” that flat pre-trends are consistent with it, and that the estimates track the true path. If that last statement is meant literally, it concedes that the estimator recovers the effect. If it is loose wording, I ask the reviewer to clarify, because the review’s conclusion depends on it. The reviewer cannot treat the pre-period as uninformative when it favours us and as the central issue when it does not. What the paragraph supports is a conditional conclusion: the estimate is informative if the identifying assumption holds, and the pre-period offers modest support for it. That is a different finding from “uninformative.”
The anticipation and timing concern is asserted, not shown, and the data cut against it. The reviewer says boards may have timed adoption to anticipated outcomes but offers no mechanism or evidence. This story has observable implications. Boards adopting in anticipation of improving outcomes would show drift in the pre-period, in e = -2 and earlier, and the pre-period is flat. Selection would also require boards to anticipate a ramp that reaches 26.6 after eight years, which is a lot of foresight. A gradual build-up with exposure is what the programme’s mechanism predicts, not what timing on a forecast would typically generate. The reviewer is also right that e = 6 to 8 rests on one to three boards, but that is a reason to discount those points, not the result. The conclusion rests on the well-populated early and middle event times, where the rise is already clear. I will show the estimates trimmed to e ≤ 5 and the confidence bands for the thin cells. What I will add to the written response. I will report results with alternative baselines (e = -1 versus the average of the pre-period) to show the pre-to-post change is invariant to normalisation. I will also re-estimate with a staggered-adoption-robust estimator (Callaway-Sant’Anna or Sun-Abraham) to head off a TWFE weighting critique, which the reviewer has not made here but may. If those results diverge from the TWFE path, I would rather find out now than in the next round.

Reply: Objection 1 Quote: “With only eight boards sharing shocks, deviations of this size are what noise would produce alone” Objection: This sentence sets 0.85 as the scale of what shared shocks produce without treatment, but the paragraph then reports post-adoption estimates rising from 1.4 to 26.6, about 30 times that scale. To answer it, the reviewer would have to show the pre-period standard errors or intervals, so the noise scale is measured rather than asserted. They would also have to show a plausible violation of parallel trends that starts at adoption and grows with exposure to the order of 26 units. Objection 2 Quote: “Flat pre-trends are evidence consistent with parallel trends. They don’t establish it, because the assumption concerns the years after adoption” Objection: This argument applies to every difference-in-differences estimate, so it cannot distinguish this one from any other, and it names no specific violation here. It also concerns parallel trends for the not-yet-treated comparison estimator, not the TWFE estimate whose identification the review disputes. To answer it, the reviewer would have to show a concrete, plausible reason these boards’ untreated paths would diverge after adoption. They would also have to connect the identifying assumption of this event-study estimator to the identification of the TWFE estimate. Objection 3 Quote: “After adoption the estimates rise with exposure and track the true path” Objection: If the estimates track the true path, the estimator recovers the effect, which contradicts the conclusion that the effect is not identified. If the phrase is loose, it is still incoherent in a paragraph that says untreated outcomes are never observed after adoption, since a “true path” cannot be known from the data. To answer it, the reviewer would have to show what “true path” refers to and how it is known. If it is a known data-generating process, they must reconcile that with the claim of non-identification. If it is only a figure of speech, they must withdraw it. Objection 2: It leaves the paragraph with no affirmative case that identification fails, but the reviewer can fall back on the burden of proof and on benchmarking against a different estimator. Objection 3: If taken literally it concedes the estimator works, but the reviewer can retreat by clarifying the wording.
